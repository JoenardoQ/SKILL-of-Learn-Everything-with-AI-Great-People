# Social phenomena learning specialization

Use this protocol for social trends, collective behavior, institutions, public controversies, cultural patterns, and policy-linked phenomena. Combine it with the learning mode selected in `SKILL.md`.

## Define the object before explaining it

Operationalize the phenomenon by specifying:

- what behavior, outcome, belief, or institutional pattern counts;
- population or actors;
- place and time period;
- comparison or baseline;
- how it is observed or measured;
- whether the goal is description, causal explanation, prediction, policy evaluation, or normative debate.

Do not reason from a vivid anecdote or an undefined label. Check whether the apparent trend could come from changed measurement, reporting, category definitions, platform visibility, or population composition.

## Separate claim types

Label important statements as:

- **descriptive:** what is happening and at what scale;
- **causal:** what mechanisms produce or change it;
- **interpretive:** what meaning participants or observers assign to it;
- **predictive:** what is expected next and under which assumptions;
- **normative:** what ought to happen and according to which values.

Evidence for one type does not automatically establish another. In particular, moral approval or disapproval is not causal evidence, and correlation alone does not identify a mechanism.

## Build a multilevel causal map

Develop competing explanations across levels rather than settling on a single story too early:

- individual: preferences, cognition, identity, skills, constraints;
- relational: families, peers, networks, status, imitation;
- organizational: firms, schools, media, platforms, bureaucracies;
- institutional: laws, norms, markets, incentives, enforcement, power;
- historical and material: path dependence, technology, demography, resources, shocks.

For each proposed mechanism, state the actors, incentives or constraints, sequence of events, expected observable evidence, time lag, feedback loops, and conditions under which the mechanism should weaken or reverse.

## Maintain causal discipline

Actively check for reverse causality, confounding, selection effects, survivorship, aggregation errors, measurement changes, and feedback between cause and outcome. Distinguish a mechanism from a label that merely renames the phenomenon.

Use comparisons and counterfactuals: ask what should differ across places, periods, groups, or policy environments if an explanation is correct. Seek evidence capable of discriminating between competing hypotheses, not only examples compatible with a favored view.

## Represent people and disagreement fairly

- Distinguish population averages from claims about individuals.
- Avoid treating demographic or cultural groups as homogeneous agents.
- Include stakeholder incentives, unequal power, distributional effects, and who bears costs or receives benefits.
- Steelman credible competing explanations and disclose uncertainty or contested definitions.
- Separate empirical disagreement from value disagreement; when values differ, name the underlying tradeoff rather than presenting it as a settled factual question.

## Teaching sequence

Treat this sequence as a phase map, not seven mandatory questions. Establish the evidence and conceptual tools for a coherent phase, then use one integrated checkpoint. Use follow-ups only for a demonstrated gap, and if the learner skips a checkpoint, continue teaching until the next meaningful phase boundary.

Across the active-learning loop, enable the learner to:

1. define the phenomenon operationally and identify the baseline;
2. separate known observations from interpretations and value judgments;
3. sketch at least two plausible mechanisms at different levels;
4. identify evidence that would distinguish them;
5. inspect confounders, selection, measurement, and feedback;
6. predict what changes under a counterfactual condition or intervention;
7. state a calibrated conclusion, strongest alternative explanation, and unresolved evidence need from memory.

For current statistics, laws, political conditions, or rapidly changing platform behavior, verify claims with current authoritative sources before teaching them as facts. Distinguish sourced facts from inference.

## Completion evidence

Do not call the phenomenon understood until the learner can define it without circular labels, distinguish description from causal and normative claims, compare at least two mechanisms, name evidence that discriminates between them, and transfer the framework to a different time, place, or population without assuming identical causes.
